# Trust, autonomy, decision framing and ethical behavioral design

## Diagnose ethical risk

Review what users believe, what is actually true, and what they can refuse, undo or inspect. Distinguish *persuasion through clarity* from *manipulation through confusion*. Key red flags: unequal action prominence, hidden fees, prechecked sensitive choices, fake scarcity, false progress, unclear data-use permissions, non-cancelable subscriptions, weaponized defaults and overconfident success messaging.

## Remediation

- **Symmetric information:** Disclose materially relevant options, costs, uncertainty and consequences before commitment. Make an informed alternative to the suggested path visible.
- **Fair framing:** Use descriptive names, neutral ordering for material choices, explain why something is recommended and avoid preselection that undermines consent.
- **Actual usability versus appearance:** Measure users' ability to finish, recover, find alternatives and understand outcomes, not only aesthetic preference.
- **Autonomy:** Provide obvious back/cancel where possible, user-directed control over attention and notifications, and reversibility when feasible.
- **Error and confidence:** For consequential operations, distinguish saved, sent, pending and failed. Maintain trustworthy receipts and clear recovery.
- **Inclusive impact:** Check design against less experienced users, users with disabilities and people with different goals and time pressures.

## Review questions

Would users choose the same thing if all options had equal prominence? Can they explain tradeoffs after selection? Is an artificial urgency cue objectively true? Does the design make them do extra work to decline, unsubscribe or protect data? If recommendation is algorithmic, is the confidence and scope clear?

## Validation

Comprehension, opt-out completion, expectation-versus-outcome, complaint/reversal and perceived trust. Use interviews alongside conversion metrics; avoid assuming low friction always signals informed consent.
